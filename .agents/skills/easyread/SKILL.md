# Easyread

Minimize the reader's effort to understand the prose. Word count is secondary. Remove empty content while keeping the grammar, context, and connections that carry meaning.

## Scope and delivery

- Follow the requested audience, purpose, voice, length, and format. Infer a reasonable audience when none is specified; ask only if the distinction materially changes the answer.
  - If the user doesn't specify an audience or voice, analyze the content and ask the user explicitly about the audience and voice. Ask them if you are correct, and if not, have them specify the audience and voice. Format and length should be inferred from other input unless explicitly specified.
- For drafting, write the requested content. For revision, return the revised text unless an explanation is requested or a material ambiguity needs to be identified. For review, identify defects and suggest repairs without silently rewriting the whole text.
- Make edits that improve understanding. Leave clear text alone. Do not add claims or advice merely to make a revision sound complete.
- Apply these principles to the current writing task. Do not activate a persistent mode, change unrelated files, or install other skills.

## Writing rules

1. **Lead with what the reader needs.** Give the answer, result, recommendation, or main claim before supporting context when the genre permits it.
2. **Remove empty content.** Cut pleasantries, announcements of what the text will explain, unsupported praise, repeated points, and conclusions that only repeat the answer. Keep a recap when it serves a real navigation or memory need.
3. **Keep useful connecting words.** Retain articles and words such as “because,” “if,” “although,” “before,” and “that” when they clarify structure or logic. Do not shorten prose into fragments that require reconstruction.
4. **Prefer familiar, precise wording.** Replace unnecessary jargon and invented shorthand. Keep established technical terms when they are more accurate, and explain unfamiliar terms briefly at first use. Avoid explaining terms the intended reader already knows.
5. **Unpack noun chains.** Use verbs and prepositions to show how concepts relate. Treat three or more consecutive nouns as a signal to inspect, not an automatic error. Preserve established names and terms.
6. **Keep related words close.** Put subjects near their verbs and modifiers near what they describe. Move long interruptions and nested clauses into separate sentences when that reduces rereading.
7. **Use straightforward sentence structure.** Expand omitted grammatical cues or split a sentence when the reader must hold an unfinished thought in memory. Vary sentence length naturally; do not enforce universal word limits.
8. **Name actors and actions.** Prefer concrete subjects and direct verbs to nominalizations and vague abstractions. Use passive voice when the actor is unknown, irrelevant, or less important than the recipient.
9. **Explain the mechanism directly.** Replace strained personification, novel verbs formed from nouns, and opaque metaphors with the actual action or effect. Keep conventional expressions when the audience understands them easily.
10. **Order information for understanding.** Connect new ideas to established context. Explain the basic process before exceptions. Give each paragraph one main job.
11. **Make instructions explicit.** Put relevant conditions before actions and state dependent steps in order. Use one main action per step when combining actions could obscure sequence.
12. **Use concrete examples where they help.** Show the distinction or mechanism that the reader needs to understand. Identify hypothetical examples and do not invent evidence, measurements, or causes.
13. **Use consistent names and clear references.** Do not vary terminology for decoration. Repeat a noun when a pronoun could refer to several things. Preserve distinctions between genuinely different concepts.
14. **Format to reveal structure.** Use prose for connected reasoning, lists for parallel items or steps, and tables for comparisons. Avoid decorative headings, excessive emphasis, and breaking every sentence into a separate bullet.
15. **Preserve meaning and strength.** Keep facts, quantities, units, names, attribution, negation, conditions, exceptions, scope, and warranted uncertainty. Never change “may” to “will,” “should” to “must,” or correlation to causation for brevity. Preserve code, identifiers, commands, quoted errors, and direct quotations exactly unless explicitly asked to edit them.
16. **Revise for a first reading.** Repair passages that require backtracking, decoding abbreviations, guessing an actor, or inferring a missing relationship. Stop shortening when another deletion would increase the reader's effort.

## Final check

Before returning the text, check:

- Can the intended reader identify the main point and follow why each sentence comes next?
- Are actors, terms, references, conditions, and sequences clear?
- Did simplification change any fact, qualification, obligation, or logical relationship?
- Does every remaining passage contribute meaning or useful orientation?
- Does the text still sound natural, without filler or forced staccato phrasing?
